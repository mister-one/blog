# The Bargain of the AI Age: Wealth, Power, and Fair Compensation

The AI age will not only transform technology.

It will transform bargaining power.

That is the deeper political and economic question beneath the rise of artificial intelligence. Not simply who builds the best models. Not simply who owns the most compute. Not simply which companies move fastest.

The real question is this:

**Who captures the value created by society’s collective intelligence?**

Because AI does not emerge from nowhere.

It is built on decades of public research, public infrastructure, human knowledge, institutional memory, open-source software, user data, cultural production, scientific discovery, and the accumulated labour of millions of people.

And yet, if we are not careful, the gains from this collective inheritance will be captured by a very small number of people and institutions.

That does not mean billionaires are evil.

It does not mean founders should not be rewarded.

It does not mean society should punish exceptional talent, ambition, or risk-taking.

But it does mean we need a better bargain.

A society that believes in merit should reward contribution.

But a society that believes in fairness should also ask whether the distribution of rewards is remotely proportional to the distribution of contribution.

The AI age forces us to confront this question directly.

## Meritocracy Does Not Mean Winner-Takes-All

A serious society must begin with a simple truth:

People are not identical.

Some people create more value than others. Some people take more risk. Some people have rarer skills. Some people make decisions that change entire industries. Some people build companies, products, systems, and institutions that create extraordinary wealth.

It would be absurd to deny this.

Jeff Bezos did not become wealthy by accident. Amazon created enormous value for consumers, businesses, investors, and the wider economy. Founders like Bezos, Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, or Nik Storonsky have built companies of extraordinary scale. Their rewards reflect, in part, the value they helped create.

A real meritocracy must recognize excellence.

It must recognize ambition.

It must recognize courage.

It must recognize execution.

It must recognize that the person who builds a trillion-dollar company has done something different from the person who performs an ordinary job inside that company.

Equal opportunity does not mean equal outcome.

But meritocracy does not require us to accept infinite inequality.

It does not require us to believe that one person is thousands, millions, or billions of times more valuable than another human being.

In nature, differences between individuals exist, but they are rarely infinite.

One marathon runner may finish in two hours while another finishes in four. The first is clearly faster. The difference is real. It matters. It should be recognized.

But nobody finishes a marathon in two hours while another takes twenty-eight years.

Human ability varies.

Human outcomes should vary too.

But the scale of economic reward in modern capitalism has become far more extreme than the scale of human difference.

That is the tension.

The problem is not that some people earn more.

The problem is that our institutions often allow certain people to capture a disproportionate share of value that was created collectively.

## Wealth Is Created Individually and Collectively

Every great company is built by exceptional individuals.

But no great company is built by exceptional individuals alone.

Amazon depends on roads, ports, logistics networks, public education, the internet, scientific research, contract law, courts, payment infrastructure, workers, suppliers, customers, and decades of accumulated technological progress.

Revolut depends not only on its founders and engineers, but on centuries of financial innovation: accounting systems, banking infrastructure, payment rails, legal frameworks, identity systems, regulatory institutions, and public trust in money.

AI companies depend on even more collective inheritance.

They depend on language itself.

They depend on books, code, images, conversations, scientific papers, public datasets, open-source communities, human feedback, and the digital traces of billions of people.

A founder may combine these ingredients brilliantly.

That act deserves reward.

But the ingredients were not created by the founder alone.

This is the core principle:

**Private genius often builds on public inheritance.**

The question is not whether founders should be rewarded.

They should.

The question is whether the rest of society should receive a fair share of the value that its own knowledge, labour, data, institutions, and history helped create.

At the moment, the answer is often no.

## The Problem Is Bargaining Power

Extreme inequality is not only a problem of morality.

It is a problem of bargaining power.

The reason a founder, executive, investor, or dominant platform can capture such a large share of value is not simply because they are uniquely productive. It is also because everyone else in the system often has weaker bargaining power.

Workers accept weaker deals.

Users give away data for free.

Suppliers become dependent on platforms.

Governments compete to attract capital.

Tax systems are full of loopholes.

Boards approve extraordinary compensation packages.

Citizens complain about inequality but rarely organize around a concrete alternative.

This is not because billionaires are uniquely evil.

It is because they bargain hard.

They ask for more.

They structure systems in their interest.

They use the tools available to them.

The deeper failure is that everyone else often fails to bargain with equal seriousness.

A founder who asks for more equity is acting rationally.

An investor who protects their downside is acting rationally.

A company that minimizes its tax burden within the law is acting rationally.

The question is why society has designed rules that make it so easy for the strongest actors to keep winning every negotiation.

The answer is not resentment.

The answer is institutional redesign.

## Fairness Requires Stronger Bargaining Rights

A fair society should not try to make everyone the same.

It should not flatten ambition.

It should not punish success.

It should not pretend that all contributions are equal.

But it should give everyone a stronger claim over the value they help create.

That means workers should have more bargaining power.

Users should have more bargaining power.

Citizens should have more bargaining power.

Public institutions should have more bargaining power.

Communities should have more bargaining power when their data, infrastructure, labour, and culture become inputs into private wealth.

In the AI age, this becomes urgent.

If a model is trained on human-created data, then the humans who produced that data should not be treated as irrelevant.

If automation displaces workers, those workers should not be told that disruption is simply the price of progress.

If public research enables private fortunes, the public should receive some return.

If centuries of institutional knowledge become the foundation for private platforms, society should not be excluded from the upside.

The point is not to stop innovation.

The point is to build a system where innovation produces shared prosperity instead of concentrated extraction.

## A Coasian Bargain for the AI Economy

There is a useful principle from economics: the Coasian bargain.

When change creates both winners and losers, society has choices.

One option is to let potential losers block progress.

That protects incumbents but prevents innovation.

Another option is to let winners take everything while losers absorb the damage.

That creates growth, but also resentment, instability, and political backlash.

A better option is to allow progress while compensating those who bear concentrated costs.

This is not charity.

It is political engineering.

Imagine a restricted taxi market. If the state liberalizes the market, consumers may benefit from lower prices and better service. New entrants may gain opportunity. But existing licence holders may lose the value of an asset they bought under the old rules.

A wise reform does not necessarily freeze the old system forever.

But nor does it simply destroy incumbents overnight.

It creates a transition.

It shares some of the gains from liberalization with those who lose from the change.

The same logic applies to AI.

AI should be adopted.

Automation should happen.

Productivity should rise.

Public services should improve.

Companies should be built.

Founders should be rewarded.

But people whose labour, data, knowledge, or institutional position helped create the conditions for that value should not be ignored.

The new bargain should be:

**Progress is allowed. But the gains from progress must be shared with those whose cooperation, data, labour, and inherited knowledge made progress possible.**

## The AI Dividend

The AI age needs an AI dividend.

Not necessarily a crude universal basic income.

Not a passive promise that people can stop participating in society.

Not a system that treats citizens as helpless dependents waiting for transfers.

The deeper idea is different.

People should not merely ask for income after value has been captured by someone else.

They should ask for rights before value is captured.

They should ask:

- Who trained this system?
- Whose data made it useful?
- Whose work is being automated?
- Whose public infrastructure made this possible?
- Whose institutions created the trust, law, education, research, and stability that allowed this company to exist?
- What share of the value should return to them?

The answer could take many forms.

Data dividends.

Worker transition funds.

Public equity stakes in companies built on public research.

Sovereign wealth funds funded by AI productivity gains.

Stronger collective bargaining.

Portable benefits.

Profit-sharing.

Training guarantees.

Tax systems that treat capital gains, loans against equity, and inherited wealth more fairly.

Public procurement contracts that require shared upside.

AI licensing regimes that compensate creators and data contributors.

The exact mechanism can vary.

But the principle is simple:

**When private wealth is built on collective inputs, collective contributors deserve a claim on the upside.**

## Taxation Should Be Simple, Fair, and Hard to Avoid

A fair bargain also requires a tax system people can understand.

The current system often feels unfair not only because some people are rich, but because the richest people can structure their affairs in ways ordinary workers cannot.

A worker receives a salary and pays tax.

A small business pays tax.

A nurse, teacher, cleaner, engineer, or civil servant pays tax.

But a very wealthy person can often borrow against appreciated assets, defer realizing gains, move wealth through corporate structures, and pass assets through inheritance systems in ways that reduce or delay taxation.

That undermines legitimacy.

People will accept inequality more easily when they believe the rules are fair.

They will not accept a system where ordinary income is taxed visibly while extreme wealth escapes through complexity.

One possible principle is radical simplicity.

Everyone should be treated more like an economic entity.

People should be able to deduct legitimate costs of earning income, as companies do.

Tax rates should be simple.

The rules should be transparent.

But the system should also prevent permanent tax avoidance through asset-backed borrowing, unrealized gains, and inheritance engineering.

The goal is not to punish wealth.

The goal is to make the bargain legible.

If society is going to reward extraordinary success, then extraordinary success must also contribute fairly to the society that made it possible.

## The Moral Error of Passive Redistribution

There is another danger.

The answer to inequality cannot only be redistribution from above.

A society where citizens merely wait for billionaires or governments to hand them compensation is not a strong society.

People need agency.

They need the confidence to bargain.

They need the institutions to organize.

They need the legal rights to make claims.

They need the cultural expectation that they are entitled to negotiate over the value they help create.

This matters because weak people get weak bargains.

If workers, citizens, users, and communities do not understand their own contribution, they will give it away cheaply.

They will accept platforms that extract their data.

They will accept compensation structures that give all the upside to executives.

They will accept tax systems that favour complexity over fairness.

They will accept automation without transition rights.

They will accept being told that progress requires their sacrifice.

A healthier society would teach people to ask better questions.

- What value am I creating?
- Who is capturing it?
- What leverage do I have?
- What rights should I demand?
- What institutions would increase my bargaining power?
- What should be negotiated collectively rather than individually?

This is not class war.

It is civic maturity.

## Founders Should Still Become Rich

A fairer system should not be anti-founder.

It should not be anti-capital.

It should not be anti-ambition.

In fact, it should do the opposite.

It should make entrepreneurship more legitimate by ensuring that success does not look like extraction.

A founder who creates a world-changing company should become rich.

Possibly very rich.

Society needs people who take risks, build institutions, create products, and push technology forward.

But there is a difference between becoming rich and capturing nearly all the surplus produced by a vast network of contributors.

There is a difference between rewarding excellence and sanctifying imbalance.

There is a difference between meritocracy and oligarchy.

In a better system, the great founder still wins.

But workers win too.

Users win too.

The public wins too.

The tax system wins too.

The communities that supplied the inputs win too.

The founder may still become a billionaire.

But the value distribution becomes more proportional, more defensible, and more socially stable.

That is not anti-capitalism.

It is better capitalism.

## From Extraction to Bargain

The central problem of the AI age is not that wealth will be created.

It is that wealth may be created from collective inputs and captured by narrow owners.

This will produce anger if the bargain is not redesigned.

People will not accept a future where their data trains the models, their jobs are automated, their public research enables the breakthroughs, their taxes fund the infrastructure, and their children inherit a society where all the upside belongs to a tiny elite.

Nor should they.

But the answer is not to stop AI.

The answer is not to demonize founders.

The answer is not to pretend everyone contributes equally.

The answer is to build institutions that make contribution visible and bargaining fair.

That means stronger worker rights.

Stronger data rights.

Fairer taxation.

Public return on public investment.

Compensation for disruption.

Shared upside from automation.

Simpler rules.

More transparent ownership.

And a culture in which citizens understand that they are not passive recipients of progress.

They are contributors to it.

## The New Social Contract for Value Creation

The AI age requires a new social contract for value creation.

It should rest on five principles.

First, excellence should be rewarded.

People who create extraordinary value should receive extraordinary rewards.

Second, contribution should be measured broadly.

Founders, workers, users, researchers, public institutions, and past innovators all contribute to the systems from which wealth is created.

Third, bargaining power should be widened.

People should have the legal, institutional, and cultural power to claim a fairer share of the value they help produce.

Fourth, disruption should be compensated.

When innovation creates concentrated losses, part of the value created should fund transition, retraining, mobility, and security.

Fifth, tax systems should be simple and fair.

Wealth should not escape contribution simply because it can hire better lawyers, accountants, and financial engineers.

This is the bargain we need.

Not equality of outcome.

Not resentment of success.

Not passive redistribution.

But a more honest meritocracy.

One that recognizes both individual excellence and collective inheritance.

One that rewards builders without allowing them to claim that they built everything alone.

One that allows progress without abandoning those who made progress possible.

The future will create enormous wealth.

The question is whether that wealth will be captured by the few or shared according to a fairer understanding of value.

The AI age will reward those who build.

But society must also reward those whose knowledge, labour, data, institutions, and history make building possible.

That is not weakness.

That is justice.

And it is the foundation of a stronger, more legitimate, more intelligent economy.
