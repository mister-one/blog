# Rebuilding the State for the Age of AI

In her 2026 RUSI speech, **“Rebuilding Britain for the new world,”** Liz Kendall argued that history rewards the nations that master the defining currency of their age. In the past, that currency was the size of a navy, the reach of railways, or the spread of power grids. Today, she argued, the defining currency is AI.

This essay starts from that insight and pushes it one step further: **if AI is now the defining currency of national power, then the state itself must be rebuilt to use it.**

There are two truths we need to face.

First: artificial intelligence is becoming the defining currency of national power.

And second: no country can truly harness AI if its state remains trapped in the operating logic of the last century.

These two truths are connected.

AI is not simply another technology. It is not just a productivity tool, a chatbot, or a new software category. It is an acceleration layer for society. It changes how we reason, how we decide, how we coordinate, how we allocate resources, how we build, how we regulate, how we defend ourselves, and how we govern.

The countries that understand this will pull ahead.

The countries that do not will find themselves with brilliant startups, powerful models, and ambitious entrepreneurs — all moving at the speed of bits — while their public institutions still move at the speed of paper.

That is the central challenge of the AI age.

Not only building better models.

Not only funding more AI companies.

Not only regulating frontier labs.

But rebuilding the state itself so that society can move at the speed of its intelligence.

## AI Is the Defining Currency of the Future

History teaches us that nations rise when they master the defining currency of their age.

At one point, that currency was land.

Then it was ships, coal, steel, railways, oil, electricity, factories, semiconductors, software, and networks.

These were never just economic inputs. They were instruments of power. The countries that mastered them built stronger economies, stronger militaries, stronger institutions, and greater influence over the future.

Today, the defining currency is AI.

AI is becoming the engine of economic power, administrative power, scientific power, and hard power. It will help cure diseases, discover materials, design weapons, detect fraud, optimize energy systems, personalize education, accelerate research, and create new companies worth hundreds of billions.

But there is a deeper point.

AI does not only make individual organizations faster. It increases the speed at which a society can perceive problems, reason about them, and act.

That means AI is not just about intelligence.

It is about institutional velocity.

And institutional velocity is now a matter of national competitiveness.

## The Slowest Institution Sets the Speed of the Country

A country can only move as fast as its slowest essential institution.

And in many countries, that institution is the state.

The state is not a side actor. It is one of the central operating systems of society. It taxes, spends, regulates, permits, procures, educates, heals, polices, adjudicates, licenses, builds, insures, and protects.

In many European countries, government spending accounts for around half of national output. So if the state does not become AI-native, the country as a whole cannot become AI-native.

Private companies may automate.

Startups may move faster.

Researchers may discover faster.

Citizens may use personal AI agents.

But if planning approvals still take years, if procurement still takes months, if courts are overloaded, if public data is fragmented, if welfare systems are opaque, if healthcare administration is manual, and if infrastructure is trapped in procedural complexity, then national progress will remain bottlenecked.

The result is a dangerous mismatch.

Our digital systems accelerate.

Our physical world does not.

Bits move instantly.

Atoms wait for permission.

This is one of the great hidden problems of the AI age: we are building technologies that can reason in seconds, inside institutions that act in years.

## The State Must Be Designed for Agents

Most digital government today is not truly digital.

It is paper logic placed behind a screen.

A PDF is not digital transformation.

A portal is not digital transformation.

A fragmented database with a nicer interface is not digital transformation.

True AI-era government requires something deeper: the state must become legible and usable by both humans and authorized agents.

That means rules should be machine-readable.

Procedures should be clear.

Public services should expose secure APIs.

Identity should be trusted.

Data should be interoperable.

Decisions should be auditable.

Citizens should be able to authorize AI agents to act on their behalf.

A citizen should be able to say:

> “Check what benefits I am eligible for.”
>
> “Renew my licence.”
>
> “Appeal this decision.”
>
> “Find the fastest legal path to open this business.”
>
> “Tell me what permits I need to build this home.”
>
> “Submit the required documents.”
>
> “Monitor my case.”

And their agent should be able to interact with the state safely, lawfully, and transparently.

Companies should be able to integrate with public systems without navigating a maze of forms and unclear rules.

Civil servants should be able to use AI to summarize cases, detect inconsistencies, compare precedents, draft decisions, model outcomes, and identify risks.

This does not mean handing government to machines.

It means designing government so that machines can handle what machines are good at — and humans can focus on what humans are good at.

Judgment.

Accountability.

Empathy.

Ethics.

Democratic choice.

Exception-handling.

Leadership.

The human role does not disappear.

It moves upward.

## Transparency Becomes National Infrastructure

If government is to use AI, transparency is not optional.

It is infrastructure.

AI needs reliable data. Citizens need understandable decisions. Public servants need systems they can trust. Courts need records they can inspect. Journalists need visibility. Parliament needs oversight. Society needs confidence.

An AI-native state cannot be a black box.

It must be measurable, auditable, and contestable.

That means public decisions assisted by AI should leave clear trails. People should know when AI is involved. They should be able to challenge errors. The state should be able to explain why a decision was made, what data was used, what rules applied, and who was accountable.

But transparency also has another meaning.

The state must become transparent to itself.

Many governments do not truly know, in real time, how well they are performing. They do not have reliable live metrics across departments. They cannot easily see where money is being wasted, where services are failing, where delays are accumulating, or where policy is not producing the intended outcome.

In the AI age, this is unacceptable.

A modern state should be metric-driven.

It should be able to take a national problem, break it down into a tree of measurable components, compute those metrics on reliable data, and use AI to interpret where the bottlenecks are.

Take a national budget.

You should be able to break it down by ministry, region, programme, outcome, supplier, cost trend, and impact. You should be able to compare healthcare spending by region, defence procurement by project, education outcomes by school type, infrastructure delivery by timeline, and welfare spending by need.

Then a reasoning layer should be able to ask:

> Where is performance deteriorating?
>
> Where is money not translating into outcomes?
>
> Where are citizens experiencing delays?
>
> Which interventions are likely to produce the largest improvement?
>
> What should be escalated to ministers?
>
> What should be handled administratively?
>
> What should be redesigned entirely?

This is not about replacing politics with dashboards.

It is about giving politics a more truthful picture of reality.

A government that cannot see itself cannot improve itself.

## AI Sovereignty Is Not Just About Models

When people talk about AI sovereignty, they often mean compute, chips, data centres, models, and national AI companies.

All of that matters.

A country that depends entirely on foreign infrastructure for intelligence will have limited control over its future. It needs strategic capacity. It needs domestic talent. It needs resilient supply chains. It needs partnerships. It needs leverage.

But AI sovereignty is not only about owning the AI stack.

It is also about having institutions capable of using AI in the national interest.

A country can have powerful models and still be institutionally weak.

It can have data centres and still have a slow planning system.

It can have brilliant AI founders and still have public procurement that blocks them.

It can have supercomputers and still have hospitals drowning in administration.

It can have frontier technology and still have a state that cannot act.

True AI sovereignty means having control over the systems that determine national outcomes.

That includes energy, compute, chips, models, data, skills, standards, security, procurement, regulation, and public administration.

But above all, it means this:

**The state must be capable of shaping AI, not merely being shaped by it.**

## The Political Problem: The State Is People

It is easy to speak about “the state” as if it were a machine.

But the state is not an abstract machine.

The state is people.

It is nurses, teachers, judges, planners, administrators, police officers, procurement managers, social workers, tax officials, inspectors, local councillors, ministry staff, and public servants who keep society functioning.

So the transformation of the state is not only a technical project.

It is a human and political project.

If AI is introduced into government as a threat, it will be resisted.

If public-sector workers believe AI means surveillance, humiliation, redundancy, or loss of status, they will slow it down. And they will not be irrational to do so. People protect themselves when change is imposed on them without a fair bargain.

That is why the AI transformation of government requires a new social contract.

Not a slogan.

A real bargain.

Public servants should be asked to embrace AI, redesign workflows, expose inefficiencies, simplify procedures, and help build a faster state.

But in return, they must share in the upside.

They should receive better tools, better training, better career paths, higher-status roles, and a fair share of productivity gains.

The message cannot be:

> “AI is coming for your job.”

It must be:

> “AI is coming to remove the worst parts of your job, increase your impact, and help you serve citizens better.”

Without that bargain, reform will fail.

Or worse, it will become theatre: AI pilots, AI press releases, AI strategies — all layered on top of unchanged bureaucratic reality.

## A Coasian Bargain for the AI State

There is a useful idea from economics: the Coasian bargain.

When a reform creates winners and losers, one answer is to let the losers block progress. Another is to let the winners take everything and force the losers to absorb the cost.

Both approaches are politically fragile.

A better approach is to define the transition clearly, allow society to move toward higher-value arrangements, and compensate those who bear concentrated losses.

Think of a restricted market, such as taxi licences.

When a government liberalizes the market, consumers benefit from more supply and lower prices. New entrants benefit from access. But incumbents may lose the value of a protected position. A possible compromise is to allow entry, but use part of the value created by liberalization to compensate incumbents during the transition.

The same logic applies to the AI state.

The wrong answer is:

> “Public-sector incumbents can block automation forever.”

Another wrong answer is:

> “AI comes in, society gets the gains, and workers absorb the disruption.”

The better answer is:

> “AI is adopted, citizens receive better services, the state becomes more productive, and public-sector workers receive a fair transition deal funded by the value created.”

That deal could include retraining guarantees, wage progression for AI-augmented roles, internal mobility, shorter working weeks where productivity allows it, transition funds for obsolete roles, and bonuses tied to measurable service improvements.

This is not charity.

It is political engineering.

If the cooperation of public servants is necessary to modernize the state, then the gains from modernization must be shared with them.

No group should have a permanent veto over national progress.

But no group should be asked to quietly absorb all the costs of progress either.

## From Bureaucracy to Civic Intelligence

The goal of AI in government should not be to replace public service.

It should be to remove unnecessary friction from public service.

Much of what public servants do today is not the highest expression of human judgment. It is repetitive, defensive, procedural work: checking forms, reconciling systems, moving information across databases, interpreting complex rules, answering the same questions, and navigating outdated processes.

AI can absorb much of this administrative burden.

But the purpose should be to elevate humans.

A teacher should spend less time on paperwork and more time with students.

A doctor should spend less time on forms and more time with patients.

A planner should evaluate more projects with better evidence.

A judge should have faster access to relevant precedents.

A tax authority should make compliance easier and fraud harder.

A welfare officer should spend less time verifying documents and more time solving human problems.

The state should not become less human because of AI.

It should become more human, because machines take on the mechanical work that currently consumes human attention.

That is the promise.

Not bureaucracy automated.

Civic intelligence amplified.

## Rebuilding the State for the New World

We are entering a world of geopolitical rivalry, technological acceleration, fiscal pressure, demographic strain, climate adaptation, and public distrust.

In that world, a slow state is not merely inconvenient.

It is dangerous.

A slow state cannot build enough homes.

It cannot approve infrastructure quickly enough.

It cannot respond to crises.

It cannot regulate fast-moving technologies.

It cannot defend itself against AI-enabled threats.

It cannot deliver public services at the quality citizens expect.

And eventually, it loses legitimacy.

Citizens do not judge the state by its strategy documents.

They judge it by whether it works.

Can I see a doctor?

Can my child get a good education?

Can I build a home?

Can I start a business?

Can I get justice?

Can I trust that taxes are spent well?

Can the government act when action is needed?

AI gives us a chance to answer those questions differently.

But only if we rebuild the state around intelligence, speed, transparency, and accountability.

That means moving from paper-era bureaucracy to AI-era administration.

From fragmented data to shared infrastructure.

From opaque decisions to auditable systems.

From static policy to live feedback loops.

From procurement as paperwork to procurement as national capability-building.

From public servants as process managers to public servants as supervisors of intelligent systems.

From government as a maze to government as a platform.

## The Choice Before Us

The choice is not between a world with AI and a world without AI.

That world no longer exists.

The choice is between a society that shapes AI according to its democratic values, and a society that is shaped by AI systems it does not understand, control, or own.

It is a choice between public institutions that rise to the speed of the age, and public institutions that become bottlenecks to national renewal.

It is a choice between fear and agency.

Between nostalgia and construction.

Between decline managed slowly and progress built deliberately.

Those who say “pause AI” are asking the wrong question.

The question is not whether AI will transform the world.

It will.

The question is whether democratic states will transform themselves quickly enough to govern that world.

Because if democratic states do not learn to use AI, others will.

Authoritarian states will use it.

Large technology companies will use it.

Criminal networks will use it.

Foreign adversaries will use it.

The answer cannot be retreat.

The answer must be control, capability, and democratic renewal.

## The New Social Contract

The AI age requires a new social contract.

Citizens should receive faster, simpler, more transparent public services.

Public servants should share in the productivity gains of AI adoption.

Companies should be able to build on public infrastructure through secure and open standards.

AI systems used by government should be auditable, contestable, and accountable.

The state should continuously measure its own performance and improve based on evidence.

And democratic governments should build sovereign capability, not to isolate themselves from the world, but to gain leverage within it.

This is how we rebuild the state for the new world.

Not by pretending technology will solve politics.

Not by allowing bureaucracy to block progress.

Not by handing public life to unaccountable machines.

But by designing institutions where humans, machines, and democratic authority work together.

The nations that win the AI age will not simply be those with the most powerful models.

They will be those with the most capable institutions.

The future belongs to countries that can turn intelligence into action.

That is the task before us.

To build a state that can see clearly.

Decide wisely.

Act quickly.

And serve people better.

A state not left behind by AI.

A state rebuilt for it.
